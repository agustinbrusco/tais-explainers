How Many Steps Fit in the Dark?

Chain-of-thought monitoring works partly because, for hard enough problems, models have to think out loud. Here is a way to see when they don't have to, and why some new architectures would change the answer.

Prototype: the core figure only, for feedback. The rest of the piece is sketched at the end.

A language model, unrolled

Each column is one token position; each dot is the hidden state at one layer. Information flows up through the layers and rightward through attention. At the top of each column the model samples a token, which becomes the next column's input.

We treat the tokens as the only readable nodes: the cards along the bottom, starting with the question, Q. That row is the transcript, and it's what a CoT monitor reads. (Hidden states can be partly read too, with probes and lenses; that comes later.)

Simplified: real models have dozens of layers (around a hundred at most in open models), and positions attend to many earlier ones (all of them, in global-attention layers). We draw 4 layers and only nearest-neighbour attention.

The longest dark path

Look for routes through the graph that never touch a card. The longest one is the most serial computation the model can do without writing anything down. Count the hidden states along it: that count is its length. Climbing straight up one column gives 4.

Attention lets a route step sideways into the next column. Can a route that zig-zags through attention touch more than 4 hidden states without touching a card?

Sideways doesn't help

No. Every attention hop also climbs a layer, so a zig-zag runs out of layers exactly when the straight climb does: it tops out at 4 too. The numbers above the graph give the longest dark path that can reach the top of each column. In a standard transformer it's the same everywhere: one trip up the stack, however many layers it has.

If you think in graphs: it's the longest path in a DAG where readable nodes reset the counter. The formal version is called opaque serial depth.

Counted differently: the formal definition counts elementary operations (about 170–190 per layer in Gemma 3) and is an upper bound on what the architecture allows; we count hidden states. And sideways steps aren't quite free: they add a small cost that grows with the logarithm of the context length (Brown-Cohen, Lindner & Shah, 2026). We leave that out of the drawing.

A problem that doesn't fit

Now give the model a task: the chain 7 → ×3 → −4 → ×2 → …, where each step needs the previous result. In this drawing each layer does one step, so one trip up the stack holds 4 of them.

The chain is 10 steps long. How many intermediate results must the model write down?

Drawing convention: one row = one arithmetic step. Real models use many layers per step: "a 100 layer model clearly can't do 100 steps in a forward pass". That's measured, not proven: the architecture's bound is in elementary operations (thousands of them), and a task with a shortcut can beat it. Even this chain has one, since its steps compose into a single multiply-and-add. Measured on chains checked for shortcuts, most frontier models manage about 4 dependent arithmetic steps without CoT, at 50% success; one outlier, GPT-6 Astra, manages about 7 (more on it below). The author calls it "heavily LLM-dependent research", with numbers that depend on researcher choices (Nanda, 2026).

Forced into text

Two. At the top of the stack, the running value has nowhere to go but into a token. The model can't carry 39 into the next trip any other way. Watch the count: each time the chain passes through a card, it starts again at 1. (The answer is written too, of course; gold marks only what the task forced out along the way.)

This is necessity: for tasks deeper than the dark path, putting intermediate results into the text isn't a habit the model might drop. It's forced. In the drawing's terms: a task needing n steps in a row, with a dark path of d, forces at least ⌈n/d⌉ − 1 intermediate results through the text.

Through the text is not the same as readable: necessity guarantees that 39 and 64 pass through the tokens, "This does not guarantee that we can understand that information" (Korbak et al., 2025). That the monitor can read them as 39 and 64 is a second assumption. The same authors argue it holds by default, since models learn a strong "natural language prior" in pretraining; it's the assumption obfuscation attacks.

Facts hit the same wall

Arithmetic is a clean test, but the wall shows up with everyday knowledge too. Here is an item from a 2026 benchmark of 4-hop questions:

"On what day of the month was the Best Actress winner at the Academy Awards ceremony whose number equals the day-of-month of the birth of the winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1992 born?"

Each hop needs the previous answer: the 1992 laureate is Derek Walcott, born on the 23rd; the Best Actress at the 23rd Academy Awards is Judy Holliday, born on the 21st.

Chained facts fare worse than arithmetic: without CoT, models manage about half as many ("approx 2x worse", Nanda, 2026). So we draw each hop as two rows, a drawing convention like the last one.

Of the three intermediate answers, which must be written down?

A bare "23"

In this drawing, only 23. Walcott is found and used inside the first trip up the stack. But 23 arrives at the top, and the next lookup needs it, so it has to surface. Holliday stays in the dark too.

Notice what the monitor gets: a bare "23". Necessity only guarantees a minimum, and here the minimum says little on its own. (A real model may write out much more than the minimum; that's propensity, not necessity. And a real model's limit is a success rate that falls off, not a hard wall.)

Measured, on different datasets (so not a trend): without CoT, Gemini 3 Pro answered 60% of 2-hop and 34% of 3-hop questions, and every model tested was at or near chance on 4 hops (Greenblatt, 2026). The 2- and 3-hop figures were with filler tokens: meaningless padding, here "1 2 3 … 300", between the question and the answer. On the benchmark above, every model except Astra gets 10% or less beyond 2 hops (Xu et al., 2026).

in two-hop questions, the first hop resolves in early layers and the second only in later ones, so the layers that are left may not hold the knowledge the second hop needs. For 32–66% of such failures, some choice of a later hidden state patched back into an earlier layer made the answer come out right: in effect, a hand-made, partial second pass (Biran et al., 2024; LLaMA 2 and 3, Pythia; not a practical method, the authors note). The next architecture makes a second pass part of the design.

Looped: the same stack, run twice

A looped (recurrent-depth) transformer reuses its layers several times per token. Same parameters, but the dark path doubles, and the same chain now forces only one written result.

Why not just add layers? Because looping turns depth into a dial. A recurrent-depth model can be run for more loops at test time to spend more compute, "unrolling to arbitrary depth" (Geiping et al., 2025). More loops, a longer dark path, no new parameters.

Status of the evidence: OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, the outlier from before, is rumored, not confirmed, to be looped; according to Redwood, OpenAI employees have denied an architectural cause. Measured, it manages about 7 dependent steps without CoT vs about 4 for the next best models (Nanda, 2026). Given filler tokens (up to 4,096 of them), it rises from about 10–20% to about 50% on the 4-hop benchmark above, and other models improve much less (Xu et al., 2026; run at low reasoning effort, with the API reporting zero reasoning tokens).

Variants: some looped designs also let a token read the deepest states of earlier tokens. Those add edges that chain the dark path across positions, not just up one column. That kind of edge is also the only way filler tokens could lengthen the dark path: in the plain graph they add width, not depth, since a route through filler columns still climbs a layer per hop. The authors don't explain Astra's large gain.

Coconut: thinking without tokens

Coconut (Meta, 2024) feeds the last hidden state straight back in as the next input: a continuous thought instead of a word. Those positions have no card to read. The number of thoughts is fixed in training and matched at inference; here there are two.

The dark path runs right through them, and the whole chain finishes with nothing readable but the answer. The depth profile shows the capacity: 12 at the last thought (and, through attention, for a few columns after it), of which this chain uses 10.

Status: Coconut is a research model, tested on GPT-2 and Llama scale, and it scored below ordinary CoT on GSM8k (34.1% vs 42.9%, GPT-2). It matters as a proof of mechanism, not as a frontier system.

Full bandwidth: text plus a private channel

Ordinary decoding throws information away: "only the sampled token returns to the bottom of the stack, while the top-layer hidden state is discarded." The full-bandwidth transformer (2026) keeps it. Each position receives the previous top-layer state, fused with the sampled token, which acts only as a gate on that state.

The model still samples a token at every position, so there is still a transcript. But the running value no longer has to pass through it. No card ever cuts the path, and the dark path grows by a full stack with every token.

Careful with "depth": the paper notes that decoding's total serial depth is unchanged, which is true, since ordinary CoT is also layers × tokens. What changes is how much of it is opaque: from about one stack to about everything generated so far.

Answer each one before touching the controls, then check.

  1. What's the smallest loop count at which a 12-step chain forces nothing into text?
    Answer3. Each pass adds 4 to the dark path, and ⌈12/(4 × 3)⌉ − 1 = 0. With 2 loops the dark path is 8, so 64 (after step 8) must be written.
  2. Why does the chain length stop mattering under full bandwidth?
    AnswerNo card ever resets the count, so the dark path grows by a full stack per token. Any chain that fits in the context fits in the dark. Tokens are still sampled, but they don't have to carry it.
  3. With Coconut, what happens to a 16-step chain once the thoughts run out?
    AnswerThe dark path reaches 12 at the last thought. The next position is an ordinary token, so the value after step 12 (374) is forced out there; steps 13–16 run in the next column.
  4. Switch to the 4-hop question. How many loops until nothing but the answer is written?
    Answer2. Four hops at two rows each need 8 in a row, and two passes give 8.
  5. Transfer: put 20 filler tokens between the question and the answer. Would a standard model now finish the 10-step chain without writing anything? What kind of edge would have to exist for filler to help?
    AnswerNo. Filler tokens are just more columns in the same graph, and a route through them still climbs a layer per hop, so the dark path stays about 4 (plus the small attention cost). They add width: room for parallel work, which is where filler tokens have been shown to help (Pfau et al., 2024). To add depth, some edge must carry a deep state of one position into a shallow layer of a later one, as full bandwidth, Coconut's thoughts, or looped variants that read earlier tokens' deepest states do.
