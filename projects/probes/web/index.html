A Ruler Through the Glass

A probe is a ruler laid through a model's hidden states: one direction and a tick. Here is what it reads, and what it only seems to read.

A ruler through the glass

Each dot is one statement, like The city of Krasnodar is in Russia., seen through the model's hidden state at its last token: 1,536 numbers, drawn here as one point. Filled dots are true statements; hollow ones are false.

A probe is a direction through those numbers plus a threshold. Train one with logistic regression and lay it through the glass: every statement's reading is how far along the ruler it falls. The readings drop through the glass onto the paper, and the tick splits them.

On 748 statements about cities the probe never saw, it reads true from false 99% of the time.

Honest geometry The plane contains the ruler's direction and the direction of largest remaining spread; the other 1,534 directions are flattened onto it. Left-right positions (the readings) are exact; heights depend on the plane we chose.

Fitting proves nothing

Now give 300 statements coin-flip labels, so there is nothing true to find, and train the same kind of ruler on them. It still splits them perfectly: training accuracy 100%.

The test is what it does on statements it has never seen. It still reads them confidently, to both sides, and gets half of them right.

labels show

With true labels the same recipe gives 100% on training and 99.5% on new statements. Only the held-out number tells the two rulers apart.

Two different controls Shuffled labels ask whether fitting means anything here (it doesn't). Hewitt and Liang's control tasks ask a subtler question: give each word its own fixed random label, and see how much a probe can score by memorizing which word is which. That share is what "selectivity" subtracts.

Side dish · optional depth

How many points can a flat cut split, any way you like?

In d dimensions, a hyperplane can split points in general position every possible way as long as there are no more than d + 1 of them. Beyond that, the share of labellings it can split falls, and it crosses one half at about 2d points: Cover's capacity of a hyperplane (1965).

Our 300 training statements in 1,536 dimensions sit at n/d ≈ 0.2, deep inside the region where any labelling fits. That's why a perfect training score says nothing here. math, not data

A second ruler

There is a ruler you don't have to train: the arrow from the average false statement to the average true one, the difference of means. It reads new statements almost as well (96%).

Yet in all 1,536 dimensions the two rulers point 59° apart. Accuracy doesn't pin down a direction: many directions separate a few hundred points in 1,536 dimensions, and each recipe picks its own.

Even the recipe's settings move it. At layer 16, turning up logistic regression's regularization swings its ruler from 67° to 42° away from the difference of means, while it keeps reading 99% of new statements.

Honest geometry The arrow is drawn where the two averages land in this plane; the angle is measured in all 1,536 dimensions, not read off the picture.

Say "not"

Take a 99%-accurate ruler at layer 12 and show it The city of Krasnodar is not in Russia. and 239 other negated statements. How often does it read them correctly?

Upside down

Each dot moves to its negated twin, and the ruler stays where it was. Filled and hollow swap sides: the ruler reads 93% of the negated statements wrong, confidently.

It isn't noise. Rank the negated statements by their readings and the false ones come out on top almost every time: an AUROC of 0.005, where 0.5 would mean no signal and 0 a perfect upside-down ranking.

It wasn't reading "true". On affirmative statements, true happens to mean "this city goes with this country", and that is what it learned. "Not" flips the meaning and leaves the match in place. (Our reading of the numbers; Marks and Tegmark proposed the same kind of feature.)

layer

At layers 8 and 16 the ruler calls nearly every negated statement false, which scores about half at the tick. The ranking underneath is still upside down (AUROC 0.02 and 0.39). So is the difference-of-means ruler's (0.03 at layer 12), even though its accuracy there looks like a coin flip. Accuracy at a tick can hide which way a ruler points.

The fix, and its limit

Train the ruler on both kinds of statement and it turns: now it reads affirmative and negated cities at 99%.

layer show

Then show it a different kind of fact, The Spanish word 'uno' means 'one'. At layer 12 it's back to a coin flip; at layer 16 it reads 73% of these and 83% of their negations. Before the fix, the same layer read 79% and 45%. The fix helped with the shift it was trained on and cost a little elsewhere: it covers the shift you thought of.

Truth has (at least) two directions

Here is why "train on both" works at all. Take the difference of means for affirmative statements and for negated ones. Their sum points along a general truth direction; their difference, along a direction that flips with "not".

Seen from that plane, the four kinds of statement sit in four groups. Truth runs left to right for both. A ruler trained only on affirmatives leans on the flipping direction, and "not" turns it upside down. Bürger and colleagues found the same two-dimensional picture in a model five times larger.
