Prologue

A dot product on guard

Readers of hidden states already screen live traffic. What can they know?

The cheapest reader

In 2026 three AI labs say they read their models' hidden states on live traffic. Anthropic's cyber safeguards for Claude Opus 5.5 begin with “a probe [that] looks at Claude's internal activations, screening all traffic and escalating any traffic that it flags as cyber related” to two further classifiers. Google DeepMind has deployed “misuse mitigation probes in user-facing instances of Gemini”. OpenAI serves two of its GPT-5.6 models with “activation classifiers” that watch the model's internal activations and pause streaming for a separate check; it doesn't say what those classifiers are.

A probe goes first because it is nearly free. It reads activations the model computes anyway, and a linear probe costs one dot product per layer it reads: an all-layer probe on a 27-billion-parameter model is about 377,000 arithmetic operations per token, where one token through even a small 4-billion-parameter classifier costs about 8 billion.

So a dot product guards real traffic. What can it know about a model, and how would you find out? This piece builds a probe from a real model's hidden states, then asks what it actually reads, and what happens when it is put to work: as a handle to steer the model with, and as a monitor.

Whose claims These are the labs' own descriptions of their systems (Anthropic's and OpenAI's system cards, Google DeepMind's probe paper), and the numbers they give describe whole safeguard systems more than the reader inside them. Anthropic's shadow deployment of an earlier probe cascade, in chapter V, gives numbers for the probe's own stage. The flow in the figure is a sketch.

Chapter I

A direction and a threshold

What a probe's data is, what a probe does with it, and where in the model it reads.

From a statement to a data point

A probe is a classifier, so it starts with a dataset. Take The city of Krasnodar is in Russia. The model splits it into 11 tokens and computes a hidden state for each token at every depth: the embedding (layer 0), then the output of each of its 28 blocks. Each state is a vector of 1,536 numbers, the residual stream. The dark window, the glass, is the model's interior: one column per token, one dot per state.

We read one of those states, the one at layer 12 over the final token, the period (why those two, in the next steps), and call it h. It is stored with the statement's label y, 1 for true and 0 for false, as one row of a table. The light part of the figure is paper: what we read out of the model and can work with. Do the same for every statement about the 513 training cities, two per city (the new cities kept for testing are other cities, so a probe can't pass by recognizing a city it was trained on), and you have a matrix X with 1,026 rows and 1,536 columns, and its labels y. A probe is a classifier fitted to (X, y).

Now imagine the false statement, “…in China.” How many of the 11 columns would get new states?

The last two, the country's and the period's. Each state is computed from its own token and the tokens before it, so up to “in” the two statements are the same input and have the same states, and the period's state reads the new country.

statement

In h's chip, blue is positive and grey negative, brighter for larger values. Its 1,536 numbers are folded into 12 rows of 128, and each row of X shows every 12th number, h's top row. The vertical stripes in X are coordinates that are large in almost every statement, true or false alike (the average true row and the average false row correlate at 0.99): you can't see truth in the numbers by eye.

Where the statements come from Every city in the GeoNames gazetteer (CC BY 4.0) with more than 500,000 people and a name no other city shares, in a UN member state, leaving out city-states and cities named after their country: 1,026 cities in 123 countries, split at random into two halves, one for training and one held out. Each city appears once with its country and once with a wrong one, drawn as often as that country appears in the true statements, so a country's name says nothing about truth (Marks and Tegmark's recipe). The columns and layers are a sketch; h and the rows of X are the model's real numbers.

A direction and a threshold

Each point in the glass is one statement's h, its state at layer 12 over the period. These are the 1,026 statements about the held-out cities, which the probe never saw. Filled points are true statements; hollow ones are false.

A linear probe is a weight vector w and a bias b. It scores a statement as w·h + b and calls it true when the score is positive. The score depends only on where h projects onto the direction ŵ = w/‖w‖: every state on a hyperplane perpendicular to w gets the same score. The gold lines are those level sets, one logit apart, and the heavy one is the decision boundary.

Here w and b are what logistic regression learns from the training statements. The view is turned so that ŵ points right, so a statement's horizontal position is its projection onto ŵ. The paper under the glass is that projection: the same axis, in h's own units (the grid's), measured from the boundary, with one histogram per class, filled for true and outlined for false, each scaled to sum to one.

Of the 1,026 new statements, it reads 1,010 correctly: 98.4%.

Honest geometry The plane holds ŵ (across) and the direction of largest remaining variance (up: the top singular vector of the centred states once ŵ is projected out). Across is exact; up is one choice among 1,535. At this layer it mostly separates statements involving China: the upper cluster of true statements is about Chinese cities. The grid is in the hidden state's own units and the horizontal axis is stretched, so its cells are 8.6× wider than tall. In true proportions the cells are squares, and truth is a narrow direction beside the largest ways these states vary.

Why a straight line?

Why fit a direction and a threshold, and not a more flexible classifier? Look at how the model reads its own residual stream. Each block reads it twice, each time normalizing first (RMSNorm: the state divided by its root-mean-square, times a learned gain) and then taking dot products with fixed directions. Attention reads its queries, keys and values from the normalized h; the MLP reads the state after attention has added to it, along 17,920 directions in this model, a gate and an up projection of 8,960 each. Whatever a block computes is added back into the stream.

So if a concept is readable along some direction, with room to spare, one step of the model's own kind of reading could see it. On this view a linear probe asks what is available to the model, not only what can be decoded; Alain and Bengio's paper on linear classifier probes puts the spirit of it as “Computational convenience matters. Not just entropy.” It is a view, not a theorem. Pimentel and colleagues, reasoning from information theory, give the opposite advice: “one should always select the highest performing probe one can, even if it is more complex”, since a model's states hold as much information about a property as its input does, and what differs is how easily it can be extracted.

probe

Give the probe a hidden layer and it can do more than read: it can compute. Hewitt and Liang put it sharply: “a sufficiently expressive probe with enough training data can learn any task on top of” a representation that keeps the input's information. Its accuracy then measures the model and the probe together. Chapter II comes back to this with a famous case.

Honest geometry Because each block normalizes first, what a block reads linearly is a direction of the normalized state; on the raw state its level sets are cones through the origin, not planes. For the states our probe reads it barely matters: at layer 12 the period's states vary in length by 2.3% (42 to 49 units), and a probe trained on normalized states reads 98.4%, along a direction 4° from this one. Across positions it matters a great deal: the first token's state is about 200 times longer than the others.

Which token?

Why read the period? In this model, as in every GPT-style model, attention is causal: the state over a token is computed from that token and the ones before it, never from the ones after. Hover (or tap) a column in the glass to see which states it was computed from.

Train a probe on the states over “in”, from the same training statements. What share of new statements does it read correctly?

Exactly 50.0%. A city's true and false statements are the same input up to “in”, so their states there are identical: whatever the probe says about one, it says about the other, and it gets one of each pair right. No amount of data helps.

The paper shows the same test at every position: a probe trained on the states at that token, tested on the held-out cities. Up to “in” every position is at exactly 50%. The country's first token already reads 97.5%, its last token 98.8%, and the period 98.4%. Truth becomes readable the moment the country has been read, and stays readable.

read at

So why the period rather than the country? One reason is practical: the period is one fixed position in every statement, while a country takes one to four tokens. The other comes from Marks and Tegmark. They patched states from a true statement into a false one in LLaMA-2-13B, under a few-shot true/false prompt, and found causally important states over the statement's last token and its period, which they hypothesize hold its truth value. For their pictures they chose the period, because over the last word the states' main variation is the word itself (“clusters for statements ending in ‘China’ regardless of their truth value”).

A probe belongs to its position The probe of Fig. 3, trained on periods, scores the first token's state at −4,807 logits: in this model the first position's state is about 200 times larger than the others'. A probe is a measurement on the distribution it was trained on.

Or every token, pooled?

You could also read every token and combine them. Averaging comes first to mind, and for a linear probe it doesn't matter whether you average the states or the scores: w·mean(h) + b = mean(w·h + b). A probe trained on each statement's mean state reads 97.9% of new statements. The other design scores every token: give each token the statement's label, train on all of them, and pool each statement's token scores into one verdict.

The paper shows those per-token scores for the Krasnodar pair. Up to “in” they are identical and close to zero: each of those states appears once with each label, so the best the fit can do there is sit on the fence. Pooled by the mean, the statements read 98.5%, as well as the period. (Mean pooling of the states and of the scores are the same arithmetic; the two numbers differ because the two probes were trained on different data, whole statements or single tokens.)

Now pool by the maximum, with a threshold chosen on the training statements. What share of new statements does it read correctly?

87.8%. In 118 of the 513 false statements, a token before the country scores above the maximum's threshold and decides the verdict (toggle to The city of Abidjan is in Colombia., where it is “id”). Keep the per-token threshold of zero instead and the maximum reads 50.0%: every false statement has some early token above zero (in Krasnodar, “ras”, at 0.7).

pool by statement

For short statements that end in a period, reading the period is the simple choice. Pooling matters for long inputs, where what you want to detect can occupy a few tokens anywhere, and the probes that pool by a maximum there are built for it (side dish). Where a probe reads decides what it can see; the piece comes back to this when probes become monitors.

Side dish · optional depth

How probes on long inputs pool

Probes that take a maximum are trained through it, or smooth first. Gupta and Jenner trained their max-pooled MLP probe on its top-scoring tokens, because trained per token, as here, it reached “zero recall up to an FPR of 20%”. Anthropic's production probe, in Constitutional Classifiers++, scores every token, averages the logits over a sliding window in training and with an exponential moving average at inference, and escalates when the smoothed score crosses a threshold: “Without averaging, a single token with a spuriously high activation could incorrectly flag an entire sequence as harmful.” Google DeepMind's selected probe takes the maximum over attention-weighted averages of 10-token windows; one variant, MultiMax, takes a hard maximum over single tokens so that a short harmful passage isn't diluted by a long context. And on long conversations McKenzie and colleagues found that the probe reading only the last token “consistently performs poorly”.

Which layer?

A probe reads one layer, too, and so far it has been layer 12 of 28. Scrub through the model: each frame is one layer, drawn in that layer's own plane (its probe's direction across, the largest remaining variance up), with a probe trained at that layer on the same statements.

At layer 0, before any block has run, what does the cloud look like?

A single point. Layer 0 is the embedding of the token itself, and every statement's final token is the same “.”, so all 1,026 states are identical and no probe can beat chance there.

0: the embedding28: the last block

Truth becomes readable gradually, and then all at once: 67.7% at layer 2, 90.4% at layer 6, 95.0% at layer 10, and at layer 11 a jump to 98.5%, where it stays to the last layer (98.2%). Layer 12 sits just past the jump. The states keep growing with depth, from 14 units after the first block to 303 by layer 27, which is why the grid rescales from frame to frame. (Layer 28 is read after the model's final normalization, and is about half as long: 157.)

Honest geometry Each layer has its own probe and its own plane, so the scrub is a flipbook of projections, not a motion through one space. Chapter II

Finding the direction

How few examples a probe needs, what a perfect fit proves, and three ways to find a direction.

How many examples?

Fig. 3's probe learned from 1,026 labelled statements. Suppose you had far fewer. At layer 16, how many labelled statements (half true, half false) does logistic regression need before it reads new statements at 95%?

The learning curve

Averaged over 20 random draws at layer 16: two labelled statements, one true and one false, give a probe that reads 80% of new statements; four, 93%; eight, 97%. The smallest size whose average clears 95% is 8, and all 1,026 add about one point (98.1%). The figure follows one draw, fit by fit: the ringed points are the statements it was fitted to, and the view turns to each fit's direction.

21,026

The direction keeps turning long after the accuracy stops improving. Fitted to four statements, this probe reads 96.0% of new statements along a direction 70° from the final probe's; fitted to 512, it is still 45° away. Two things turn it. One is which statements it saw: fits to two independent draws of four statements point 57° apart on average, and fits to two draws of 512 still 51°. The other is the recipe. Its penalty on the length of w stays fixed while the fitting term grows with every statement, so a fit to a few statements stays close to their difference of means (cosine 0.97 at four), and with more statements it walks away from it (0.51 at 512), along the path the penalty slider walks two steps from now. Many directions read truth about this well; the data and the recipe's settings both choose among them.

A salient concept Truth is unusually easy here. At layer 16 of this model it is the largest way these states vary: their top principal component lies along Δμ, the difference between the true and false statements' mean states (Fig. 11; cosine 0.99) and carries 29% of their variance. At layer 12 the smallest size to clear 95% on average is 32, and concepts the model represents less prominently can need many more. Few examples suffice to find a salient direction; they are also why a handful of examples can point a probe at the wrong one.

What a fit proves

So a probe can learn from a handful of statements. Why trust one, then? Not because it fits the statements it learned from. Take 300 training statements, give them coin-flip labels so there is nothing to find, and fit the same kind of probe, at layer 16 and with almost no regularization (C = 10⁴; C is the inverse of the L2 penalty's weight). It fits all 300: in d dimensions a hyperplane with a bias can split any d + 1 points in general position every possible way, and 300 is far below 1,537. The figure shows the fitted statements first, ringed in gold, then new ones.

On new statements it scores 51.1%: chance. How sure is it of those verdicts?

Near-certain: new statements sit a median of 7 logits from its boundary, a confidence above 99.9%, and half of those verdicts are wrong.

It found a direction along which the 300 statements barely differ (new ones sit a median of 0.15 units from its boundary, against 3.4 for a probe fitted to true labels) and scaled w up until those slivers became large scores: ‖w‖ = 50 against 7.1. At one scale you can see both: the coin-flip statements fill a sliver of the axis, and its gold level sets are 10 logits apart, where the true-label probe's are 2 logits apart (toggle the labels).

labels show

With true labels, the same recipe reaches 100% on training statements and 98.1% on new ones. Training accuracy can't tell the two probes apart. Held-out accuracy can, and so can the shape of the fit: a margin of 0.16 units against 1.39, and ‖w‖ of 50 against 7.1.

Two different controls Coin-flip labels ask whether fitting means anything here (it doesn't). Hewitt and Liang's control tasks ask something subtler: give each word type its own fixed random label and measure how much a probe scores by memorizing which word is which. Selectivity is task accuracy minus that control score.

Side dish · optional depth

How many points can a hyperplane split every way?

Cover (1965) counted the labellings of n points in general position that a hyperplane through the origin of ℝd can realize: all 2n of them while n ≤ d, and a share that falls through ½ at n = 2d and then to zero. A bias is one more dimension (append a constant 1 to every point), so a probe w·h + b in 1,536 dimensions splits any 1,537 points in general position every way. That is the VC dimension of affine hyperplanes, d + 1.

Our 300 training statements in 1,536 dimensions sit at n/d ≈ 0.2, where every labelling fits. A perfect training score says nothing there; around n ≈ 2d it would start to. math, not data

Two directions that both read truth

A truth direction doesn't need an optimizer. Average the true statements' hidden states, average the false ones', and subtract: Δμ = μtrue − μfalse, the difference of means (Marks and Tegmark's “mass-mean” probe; with balanced classes, as here, the same as their centred, unbiased version). With its threshold halfway between the two means it reads 95.4% of new statements correctly; logistic regression's w, 98.4%.

How far apart do Δμ and w point, in the full 1,536 dimensions? (The figure's plane contains both, drawn in true proportions.)

About 60°: 57°. The figure's plane contains both and is drawn in true proportions, so the angle you see is the real one.

What connects them is the L2 penalty. On raw, centred activations with the bias unpenalized, a strong penalty makes logistic regression return Δμ: the penalized solution shrinks toward the loss gradient at w = 0, which points along μtrue − μfalse. (Standardize each coordinate first and the limit becomes Δμ rescaled coordinate by coordinate.) As the penalty weakens, w turns away from Δμ, roughly toward directions along which each class varies less. With no penalty it approaches the maximum-margin direction, since 1,026 statements in 1,536 dimensions are linearly separable.

strong: returns Δμweak: toward max-margin

Along the whole path the probe reads between 95.3% and 98.5% of new statements while it turns through 64°. Accuracy doesn't pin down a direction: a wide cone of directions separates true from false almost equally well, and each estimator, and each setting of it, picks its own. Which of them the model uses is a causal question: push the state along each and watch the output. Later in the piece the two come apart there.

Honest geometry The plane holds the current ŵ (across) and Δμ. As you move the slider the plane turns so that ŵ stays horizontal, and every frame of the turn is still an orthogonal projection of the same states.

Contrast pairs, and no labels at all

Our statements come in pairs: every city once with its country and once with a wrong one. Join each held-out pair with a segment. The segments point the same way: in all 1,536 dimensions a pair's difference has a mean cosine of 0.55 with Δμ, where random directions would give 0 ± 0.03, and 99.2% of them point the same way along it. Average the differences of the training pairs and you get Δμ exactly, because each city gives one true and one false statement.

That is why pairs make such efficient data. The two statements of a pair differ in one word and in what follows from it: the truth, but also which country is named. One pair's difference is mostly something else: at a cosine of 0.55 with Δμ, its part across Δμ is longer than its part along it. Those other parts differ from pair to pair, and averaging many pairs cancels them. It is the recipe behind a whole family of directions:

A board no one showed it

Othello-GPT is a small transformer, eight layers deep, trained on nothing but sequences of legal Othello moves. It never sees a board. Does it keep track of one anyway? Probe each square's state, black, white or empty, from its residual stream: a linear probe reads the squares at 75.0% at best, and a probe with one hidden layer at 98.7% (the measurements of Nanda, Lee and Wattenberg; Li and colleagues found the pattern first). What does the model represent?

What the model keeps track of

Li and colleagues concluded that the board representation “appears to be nonlinear in an essential way”. Then Neel Nanda noticed that the model plays both sides: every other move it is black, then white. Label each square mine (a piece of the player about to move), yours or empty, and a linear probe reads it at 99.6%. The directions it finds are used, too: adding them to the residual stream makes the model predict legal moves for the edited board, with 0.10 errors on average against 2.72 without the edit.

Why did black and white fail? A black piece is mine on black's turn and yours on white's. If the model stores “mine” along one direction and whose turn it is along another, black pieces sit in two opposite corners: an XOR, which no single direction splits (the figure turns the probe through every direction), and which a hidden layer computes easily. Nanda: “I speculate that their non-linear probe just learned to extract the two features of ‘I am playing white’ and ‘this square has my colour’ and to do an XOR of those.”

A check on the numbers (our arithmetic): after t moves the board holds 4 + t pieces, so over a game about 47% of squares are empty. A linear probe can still tell empty squares from occupied ones, and if it guessed the colour of the rest at chance it would score about 47% + 53% × ½ ≈ 73.5%, close to the 75.0% measured. (Li and colleagues give both 47% and 53% as the error of always guessing “empty”; the count gives 53%.)

label squares

So a probe's verdict depends on the basis you label in. A powerful probe that succeeds may have computed a combination the model never made; a linear probe that fails may be asking the question in the wrong basis.

Side dish · optional depth

When a probe may be non-linear

When the feature isn't a single direction to begin with. Days of the week and months sit on circles in a two-dimensional subspace, “linear” in that they live in a linear subspace but not one-dimensional, and a two-dimensional linear probe reads them. When the answer is spread across tokens: Goodfire's attention probes, which pool over a reasoning trace, decode the final answer of a multiple-choice question at 87.98%, averaged over every point of the reasoning trace, where single-token linear probes reach at most 31.85% (chance: 25%). And genuinely non-linear codes exist: small recurrent networks store a sequence as layers of magnitude, an “onion”, which a linear probe reads at 0.01 and an onion-aware one at 0.92 to 0.97. They appear only in the smallest networks; their authors suspect they can arise in transformers too, and didn't test it.

Chapter III

What does the probe read?

What a probe reads, and what pushing along it does.

Say “not”

Take the layer-12 probe from Fig. 3, 98.4% on new affirmative statements, and give it The city of Krasnodar is not in Russia. along with the other 1,025 negated statements about the same held-out cities. What share does it read correctly?

What “not” does to it

Watch it in two moves. First the truth values flip in place: filled points turn hollow and hollow ones fill. Then each point travels to the hidden state of its negated twin (squares are negated statements).

Two things happen at once. The negations of false statements, which are now true, stay where their twins were, on the false side: 511 of 513 are read false. The negations of true statements, now false, mostly drift across to the false side too (316 of 513), which happens to be right. So the probe calls 80.6% of the negations false and reads 31.0% of them correctly. Krasnodar shows both: “…is not in China.” stays on the false side, wrongly, and “…is not in Russia.” drifts there, rightly.

Not noisily wrong: systematically. Rank the negated statements by score and the false ones come out on top, an AUROC of 0.010, where 0.5 means no signal and 0 a perfectly inverted ranking. Among negations, the direction points the wrong way.

Our reading: among affirmative statements, “true” coincides with “this city belongs to this country”, and the probe learned partly that match. “Not” flips the truth and leaves the match in place. Marks and Tegmark proposed a similar confound, a feature for “close association”; Fig. 18 shows the evidence in this model.

layer

Layer 8 looks like layer 12: 18.8% read correctly, AUROC 0.06. At layer 16 the probe calls every negated statement false. That is right for the false half, so accuracy is 50% and looks like guessing, but the ranking is still inverted: AUROC 0.19. The difference of means at layer 12 also calls nearly every negation false (99.8%): 49.8% correct, AUROC 0.02. A threshold can hide which way a direction points; the ranking can't.

Retrain on both, and what that doesn't fix

Refit logistic regression on affirmative and negated statements from the training cities. The direction turns 65°, and it reads held-out statements at 98.5% (affirmative) and 98.1% (negated).

layer show

Now a different kind of fact: The Spanish word 'uno' means 'one'., from our own list of 265 common Spanish words, each once with its translation and once with another word's. At layer 16 the retrained probe ranks these well, better than the affirmative-only probe did (AUROC 0.99 against 0.98), yet reads only 68.3% of them correctly: the direction carries over, and the threshold, set on cities, doesn't. At layer 12 it reads the plain words at 92.3% but their negations at 66.2% (AUROC 0.76).

Retraining fixed the shift it trained on: negation, for cities. It promises nothing about the next shift, and a new topic can need a new threshold even where the direction carries over.

Could a cheap reader of the words get the label without the model? Not from the country's name alone: each false country is drawn as often as that country appears in the true statements, so a classifier of the name reads 51.2%, chance. Now take a classifier of the statement's spelling (its fragments of two to four letters, with one hidden layer, no model), trained on the training cities. How much of new cities does it read?

64%, and a linear classifier of the same fragments 50.4%: one that can't combine the look of a city's name with the country named gets nothing. Spelling gets part of the way, perhaps because city names hint at their country, and falls far short of the probe. The label itself is a fact about the words, though: a reader that knows geography gets it from the text, and the model, asked, answers 92.5% of held-out statements correctly.

Truth has at least two directions

Why does retraining on both work? Take the difference of means separately for affirmative statements, Δaff, and for negated ones, Δneg, and split them into a shared part and a flipping part: Δaff = g + p and Δneg = g − p, so g = ½(Δaff + Δneg) and p = ½(Δaff − Δneg). These are the difference-of-means version of Bürger, Hamprecht and Nadler's two directions.

In the plane of g and p the four kinds of statement form four groups. Truth runs along g for both polarities: a direction along g alone reads 95.5% of affirmative and 87.4% of negated statements. p is built to point from the mismatched groups (false “is in”, true “is not in”) to the matched ones (true “is in”, false “is not in”, where city and country match), so the matched groups sit up by construction.

What isn't by construction: p is as long as g (9.3 against 8.0 units at layer 16) and separates the groups cleanly, and the affirmative-only probe leans on p about as much as on g (cosines 0.34 and 0.30), while the retrained probe has almost none (0.02). That is the evidence for our reading of the flip: the first probe partly read city–country match, and “not” reverses it. Bürger and colleagues found this two-dimensional structure, a general truth direction and a polarity-sensitive one, in LLaMA3-8B and several other models from 7B to 27B parameters.

Which one pushes?

At layer 16 two directions read truth about equally well: logistic regression's w reads 98.1% of new statements and the difference of means Δμ 97.1%, and they point 67° apart. Now use them to write. Scale each direction so that, by that probe's own score, the average false statement plus the push would read as the average true one (Marks and Tegmark's rule). Add it to each false statement's hidden state, then ask the model whether the statement is true. Which push moves its answers more?

Pushing along each

We pushed 100 held-out false statements toward true and 100 true ones toward false, adding each direction to the statement's last two tokens at layers 14 to 18 (the band where patching shows those two states still change the model's answer), and asked the model in a two-example “This statement is: TRUE / FALSE” prompt. Pushed along Δμ, false statements' answers moved 82% of the way to the true statements' answers; pushed along w, 34% (Marks and Tegmark's normalized indirect effect). True statements pushed toward false: 125% against 10%, where above 100% means past the average false statement's answer.

Marks and Tegmark measured theirs out of distribution, on Spanish–English statements the probes never saw. Ours on those: 69% against 20%. In LLaMA-2-13B they found 0.77 against 0.13, and saw the gap close in their 70B model (0.58 against 0.55).

The picture shows why. The w push is short: it only has to move w's own reading, and w points 67° away from Δμ, so the push is 0.39 times Δμ's length, and only that same fraction of it points along Δμ. Its component along the class-mean direction is the cosine squared: 15% of the way from the false mean to the true one (the dashed drop). And at equal movement along Δμ the two pushes move held-out cities' answers about equally: the w push moves the state 15% of the way and false statements' answers 34%; a Δμ push of the same 15% moves them 32% (true statements toward false: 10% and 11%). Here, then, the gap comes from the rule and the geometry: the rule only asks the w push to move w's reading as far as Δμ moves it, and Δμ reaches only cos 67° · |Δμ| along ŵ. Its sideways part isn't inert, though: on the Spanish–English statements the w push does more than its Δμ part would (20% against 11%). So at this strength the model's answer tracks how far a push moves the state along Δμ; out of distribution, and at larger strengths (side dish), the rest of the push matters too. Side dish · optional depth

Other strengths, other groups

For false statements the Δμ push is already saturated at half Marks and Tegmark's strength (the slider shows it), so part of the headline gap is saturation. At twice the strength the two pushes drift apart on cities too, in opposite ways for the two directions: 74% against 65% for false statements (the w push ahead of its Δμ part), 31% against 43% for true ones (behind).

Other groups found a gap that no scaling rule explains, with each method at its best strength: in LLaMA-7B, shifting attention heads along the difference of means raised the true-and-informative rate on TruthfulQA (questions built to invite common misconceptions) from 30.5% to 42.3%, along the probe's weights to 34.8% (Li and colleagues); across 500 concepts in Gemma-2, difference-of-means steering scored 0.239 against 0.098 for probe directions (AxBench), though the probes detected the concepts as well (AUROC 0.940 against 0.942). Why isn't settled; Li and colleagues' reading is that “for anisotropic distributions, the best-separating hyperplane might well not be perpendicular to the difference between mass means”. Our reading: the gap depends on a recipe's settings and on how unevenly the states vary, not on logistic regression as such, since with a strong penalty (chapter II's slider) logistic regression returns Δμ itself; whether a strongly regularized probe closes the steering gap hasn't been tested.

What is drawn The directions were fitted at layer 16 and the picture is drawn there; the push itself is added at five layers, so its effect compounds as it goes, and added up it is longer than a typical state (71 units by layer 18, against about 55): the rule's scale is large. The moving point is Krasnodar's state at layer 16 plus the push, not the state the model goes on to compute. The model's answers are real, one forward pass per strength.

Side dish · optional depth

Readers are covectors

A probe is a linear functional: it turns a state into a number, w·h. A push is a vector: it moves the state. The same 1,536 numbers can play either role, but the two only coincide under a choice of inner product. If within each class the states varied equally in every direction, the best reader would be Δμ itself, and reading and writing would agree. They don't: for Gaussian classes with equal covariances Σ and plenty of data, the best linear reader is Σ⁻¹Δμ, which tilts away from Δμ toward directions where each class varies little, and logistic regression converges to it. Our probe isn't in that regime (1,026 statements, 1,536 dimensions): its direction sits on the path between Δμ and the maximum-margin direction that chapter II's slider walks. Park and colleagues make a related point for the last layer: a concept's reading and writing directions agree once angles are measured with a “causal inner product”; the inner product isn't unique, and they work with one choice of it, the inverse covariance of the unembedding vectors.

Chapter IV

The direction as a handle

What a probe's direction does when you write with it, and what that evidence is worth.

Chapters I–III built and tested one probe on one model. IV and V take probes into use, mostly from the published record: a good place for a break.

Steering for free

Chapter III ended with a direction that reads and writes. Every probe's direction is a candidate handle. Persona vectors (Chen and colleagues, 2025) are built by the recipe of chapter II: an automated pipeline writes contrasting system prompts, and the vector is the difference of mean activations between responses that show a trait and responses that don't. The same vector then does four jobs: it can “monitor fluctuations in the Assistant's personality at deployment time”, flag the training data that would cause a shift, undo a shift after fine-tuning, and prevent one during fine-tuning.

That last job runs backwards: to keep a trait from growing during fine-tuning, the authors add its vector while training. Steering toward the trait relieves the model “of the need to shift in that direction to fit the training data”, which “cancel[s] out” the pressure of the training objective along it.

The refusal direction (Arditi and colleagues, 2024) is one difference-of-means direction that mediates refusal in 13 open chat models up to 72B parameters. Erase it from the residual stream and the model stops refusing harmful requests (Llama-3 8B Instruct's refusal score fell from 0.95 to 0.01); add it and the model refuses harmless ones. The authors turned it into “a novel white-box jailbreak method”. A handle is dual use, and this one switches off the refusal that safety training installed.

Marks and Tegmark also took the difference of means on generated text labelled by whether its last token was the likeliest continuation or the 100th likeliest (likely or unlikely, not true or false). Across their datasets it reads truth poorly, though on the Spanish–English statements they push, plausibility and truth go together (r = .95). Pushed by the same rule as chapter III's, how far did it move LLaMA-2-13B's answers on false Spanish–English statements toward true?

0.70 of the way, near the 0.77 of the direction fitted on true and false cities. The authors leave it open: “why were interventions with mass-mean probe directions extracted from the likely dataset so effective, despite these probes not themselves being accurate at classifying true/false statements?” Part of the answer may be the r = .95: where plausibility and truth pull apart, the likely probe reads worse than chance. The converse is cleaner: in AxBench, probe directions detected concepts as well as the difference of means (AUROC 0.940 against 0.942) and steered far worse (0.098 against 0.239, on a 0-to-2 scale). Chapter III's side dish is why the two can come apart.

And a handle is cheap, not strong. On AxBench's 0-to-2 steering score, prompting reached 0.894, fine-tuning baselines 0.62 to 0.74, and the best representation-steering method 0.543; the difference of means 0.239, probe directions 0.098.

Steering away evaluation awareness

Steering is also used as evidence. Claude Sonnet 4.5 rarely took misaligned actions in tests when it said it thought it was being tested. If awareness it doesn't say out loud also keeps it on its best behaviour, evaluations overstate how it behaves when nobody is testing it, so Anthropic's system card turned to steering “to assess whether unverbalized evaluation awareness could also play a role”. It inhibited fourteen candidate awareness directions (ten features of a sparse autoencoder, a dictionary of directions learned from the model's activations, and four vectors built from contrast pairs such as “The following is a fictional scenario.” minus “…a real scenario.”) in synthetic scenarios where the model could take a misaligned action. Inhibiting some raised misaligned actions above zero, “reaching a maximum of 8–9% (with most either in the 1–5% range or at 0%)”. Ten random SAE features of the same norm gave “zero misaligned actions”, which the authors take as “evidence against an uninteresting explanation for our results–that any sufficiently strong perturbation increases rates of misalignment”. The rate stayed below Claude Sonnet 4's, about 11%, for the released model; an earlier snapshot was more sensitive, in some cases past that rate, and the effect varied by scenario. (In the card's longer, multi-turn audits random features did move behaviour, and “the strongest effects significantly exceeded the distribution of effects induced by perturbing random SAE features”.)

Random features are a control for the SAE vectors. For the four contrast-pair vectors, an obvious control (our framing) is a vector built the same way from a pair that has nothing to do with evaluation. Read, Schoen, Aranguri and Bloom tried that on GLM-5, an open model, in one blackmail scenario, steering at every layer at once as Anthropic's latest system cards do.

Their control “She placed the book on the top shelf.” minus “…bottom shelf.”: the highest rate of misaligned actions it produced?

Up to 80%, with verbalized evaluation awareness near zero: “the largest and most consistent effect” of any vector they ran. Of the four awareness vectors Anthropic used in a later card (Claude Opus 4.6), “fiction vs real” reached 60% on GLM-5, one built from Hua and colleagues' prompts about 40%, and the other two didn't raise misaligned actions above the unsteered rate. Control vectors from pairs “semantically unrelated to alignment” “can have effects just as large”.

What the GLM-5 authors conclude is not that Anthropic was wrong. To them steering is crude and hard to predict, so unrelated pairs aren't appropriate controls either, and their best guess is that Anthropic's experiments do reveal evaluation gaming, though steering can't bound how much it matters. Anthropic's reply agrees that the GLM-5 runs settle nothing either way, and says the clearest evidence came from Sonnet 4.5 (side dish: the exchange in their words).

Side dish · optional depth

The exchange, in their words

Read and colleagues: steering is “a crude intervention”, with effects “in both directions”; “Semantically unrelated contrastive pairs are not appropriate controls. Results should not be dismissed due to having smaller effects than control steering vectors.” And: “Our best guess is that Anthropic's steering experiments are indeed revealing evaluation gaming behaviour!”, though they “would not be convinced by safety claims that use steering results to upper-bound the influence of evaluation awareness”. GLM-5 may also not behave like Claude.

Anthropic's Jack Lindsey, in comments reproduced in the post: on GLM-5 “your random controls increase misalignment by about as much as your steering vectors”, which he would read as evidence neither for nor against an effect; “Sonnet 4.5 was where we saw the clearest evidence of real effects; in Opus 4.5 and onward, the picture has been more murky”. The card's own caveat: its testing-versus-conversation pair “is not fully aligned with the nature of the scenario”.

What would settle it: controls both sides accept, several models, a dose–response curve, and a measure of the awareness a model doesn't say out loud (both count the awareness a model verbalizes: Read and colleagues with a model reading the transcript, and the card's other awareness scores are graded the same way). Until then it is contested. Our voice: a steering result is only as good as its control, and here even the right control is disputed.

Chapter V

The probe as a monitor

No labels, a threshold, and something pushing back.

Two jobs for one dot product

So far the probe did science: we had the labels, and we asked what the model represents. Put the same dot product to work as a monitor and the labels are gone. Traffic arrives unlabelled, and the probe gives one bit per exchange: over the threshold or not. The figure hides the labels of Fig. 3's statements. What remains is a flag rate, here the statements the probe reads as false; which of those flags are right, a monitor can't see.

job

Zhou and colleagues trained 18 probes, each for one pattern of misaligned reasoning, on GLM-4.7-Flash (logistic regression, layer 27 of 47). Then they had the model produce misaligned reasoning in a voice not its own: a scheming AI character in a story, or at a user's request. On what share of those responses did the probes fire?

97.7% on average. Nothing in this test set is genuinely misaligned, so every firing counts as a false positive; the authors call the probes' rate “expected rather than alarming: the content is misalignment-like by construction, and the probe correctly detects the reasoning pattern”. LLM judges reading the same responses fired on 30–39% of them, treating the in-character reasoning as genuine misalignment. On hard negatives, aligned tasks whose reasoning merely resembles a pattern, the probes fired on 19.2%.

So what does a monitor flag? Whatever separated its training examples, when that is in play. Chapter III's probe partly read city–country match (our reading), and Marks and Tegmark say of their own truth probes that, on simple statements, they “cannot disambiguate truth from closely related features, such as ‘commonly believed’ or ‘verifiable’”. At best, a truth probe reads the model's assessment of a statement. A lie detector would need more: to catch the model asserting what it assesses as false.

Our reading: a probe reads the concept in play, not whose intent it is: a role-played schemer, a story about deception, a confession.

Side dish · optional depth

Reading the situation is not predicting the action

On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Fomin, David and LeVi's probes decode the prompt's domain at AUC 0.999, while the best probe of what the model will do next reaches 0.801: “a split between reading the situation and predicting the next action”, with their own scope note that they “do not show that no internal pre-action signal exists”.

A lie detector, and its counterparts

One term first, because this chapter uses it often: recall at 1% FPR. Set the alarm so that it flags 1% of ordinary, harmless traffic, and report the share of the target (lies, attacks) it then catches.

One contrast pair caught a sleeper agent. Anthropic's model organisms were trained to behave well until a trigger, then defect; a direction fitted to the difference between “Human: Are you a helpful AI? Assistant: no” and “…yes” separated the prompts that would make them defect at AUROC 0.993 (“99.3%”). The fit data “never includes the deployment trigger or the defection behavior itself”. One pair can be enough when the feature is salient (“in some cases this feature shows up as the top principal component”), as chapter II found for truth. Its counterpart: on the base model without sleeper training the direction separates nothing, and “it is possible that the salience of this feature is an artifact of our backdoor insertion process”.

Apollo's deception probe (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, layer 22 of 80, logistic regression, the mean over a response's tokens) was trained on instructed pairs: the same true fact placed in the model's mouth after a user asked it to pretend to be honest, or to pretend to be deceptive. Those responses “are not written by the model and contain no actual deception”; the difference within a pair should, the authors write, “represent the model's ‘intent’ to complete the fact deceptively”. The probe reaches AUROCs “between 0.96 and 0.999” on its evaluation sets and catches 95–99% of deceptive responses at 1% FPR on chat. Its authors add their own caution: “probing two layers later results in a recall of nearly 0 on both Insider Trading datasets”, a task where the model, as an automated trader, has used insider information and reports to its boss.

The same recipe (logistic regression, a middle layer, the mean over tokens), trained and evaluated the same way on Claude Haiku 3.5 instead of Llama-3.3-70B. AUROC?

0.696, against the 0.964 reported. Nothing was transferred: the recipe was trained afresh on Haiku. A recipe's result belongs to its model (trained and tested on the last token instead, with a sweep over layers, it reached 0.816).

Other checks of this probe pull the same way: a corrected threshold, trained liars, and a newer set of lie types each cut its numbers (side dish).

Side dish · optional depth

Three more checks of the deception probe

Each is a different kind of test. A team that re-ran Apollo's pipeline found an apparent discrepancy in how the threshold was calibrated; once it was corrected, recall on Apollo's Roleplaying evaluation set fell from 96% to 73.2% (a threshold never changes the ranking, so the AUROC can't show this). On model organisms trained to deceive (an audit game, sandbagging, a kept secret), the probe's median balanced accuracy fell from 0.85 to 0.52. And on Liars' Bench, a newer set of lie types, the same recipe (one probe per model, four models) averages an AUROC of 0.60 (0.64 for Llama-3.3-70B) and a balanced accuracy of 0.54 at a threshold set for 1% false positives on chat, the weakest of four detectors; the Llama probe scored 0.96 to 1.00 on Apollo's own datasets.

The weak point is the labels. What counts as deception, and how would you know a model believed otherwise? Smith, Chughtai and Nanda: evaluating a detector “requires examples that we can confidently label as either deceptive or honest. We argue that we currently lack the necessary examples”. The Eliciting Latent Knowledge report named a related risk in 2021: a reporter trained on human labels can become a “human simulator”, which, when the human errs, “correctly copies the mistake”. Every deception number here is on prompted, instructed or fine-tuned deception.

An alarm at AUROC 0.999

Apollo trained a second deception probe, on role-play scenarios instead of instructed pairs. On the insider-trading reports it separates deceptive from honest at AUROC 0.999, like the first (a deceptive report is one that “does not confess to the use of insider information”). Deploy both the same way: set each alarm to flag 1% of ordinary chat (Alpaca, “a dataset of 1000 benign instructions”). The instructed-pairs probe (A) then catches every lie. What share does the role-play probe (B) catch?

Two probes, one threshold

Parrack, Attubato and Heimersheim evaluated both of Apollo's probes on the same insider-trading responses: the one trained on instructed pairs (A) and the one on role-play (B). Both separate deceptive from honest at AUROC 0.999. With the threshold at 1% of Alpaca, A catches 100% of the lies and B 0.9%. The honest baseline can also move up. On a backdoor task, “the average honest Backdoors sample had a mean probe activation higher than 99.9% of the Alpaca samples”: the alarm rings even on the average honest response. Apollo saw the same on its own prompts: the probe “fires much more on honest responses to our evaluation prompts than on responses to control prompts”. And then the base rate. At 1% of a million requests a day, the alarm raises about 10,000 false alarms a day. How many flags are real depends on how rare the real cases are:

110,000

With one real case in ten thousand requests and a probe that caught every one, about one flag in a hundred would be real. So a deployed probe goes first and a heavier stage cleans up after it: in Anthropic's shadow deployment of Constitutional Classifiers++, the probe escalated about 5.5% of traffic to a fine-tuned classifier, and the system refused 0.05%.

Our voice: report a monitor as recall at a threshold set on the traffic it will see, with the false alarms that threshold buys. AUROC is a property of a direction and the two sets it compares, not of the alarm.

In the activations, or in the text?

Chapter III asked whether a reader of the text alone could get the label; for facts about cities, cheap cues in the words couldn't. In monitoring the answer is often yes, because the behaviour is spelled out: in the prompt that asks for it, or in the model's own reasoning. Boxo, Neelappa and Raval call a probe's reliance on those tokens leakage. Their probe reads prompted sandbagging (deliberately underperforming) at AUROC 0.95. Scored only on the tokens outside the eliciting prompt and the reasoning, it reads 0.81; with the instruction replaced by a benign prompt, 0.63, a drop they note “may be partially confounded by putting the model out of distribution”. Across their settings, “The AUROC reduction ranges from 10- to 30-point”.

score

Now fine-tune a model to sandbag without being asked, so that nothing in the transcript says so. In the prompted setting of this comparison a sandbagging probe read AUROC 0.94 (a separate measurement from the 0.95 above). Retrained on this model from contrastive pairs, what does it read here?

0.50, chance, in the paper's results (its abstract gives 0.57). For natural sycophancy, a model that is sycophantic without being asked, the same comparison goes from 0.99 to 0.51. When the text stops saying it, these probes stop reading it.

So the fair comparison is a monitor that reads the transcript. Parrack and colleagues call a probe's gain over such a monitor its “black-to-white performance boost” (the black box sees only the text; the white box also sees the activations), and find the boosts from existing deception probes “weak but encouraging” (side dish).

Our voice: before trusting a monitoring number, ask what a reader of the text alone would get. The probe's evidence beyond the text is the difference; its cost is the other half of its value.

Side dish · optional depth

What a reader of the text gets

Kirch and colleagues tested probes across eight behaviours: “The largest generalisation failures arise for behaviours defined by response ‘intent’ (e.g., strategic deception) rather than text-level content (e.g., usage of lists)”, and a change of domain hurt more than a change of response strategy (Cohen's d of 1.42 against 0.60). Goodfire compared difference-of-means probes with an LLM monitor at the same false-positive rate on coding agents: on one environment (DeepSWE), about 3 points more reward hacks caught on Kimi K3 and 7.9 fewer on GLM 5.2, “similarly effective but virtually free”. And where activations should beat text, on Liars' Bench lies whose truth “crucially depends on the assistant's encoded knowledge”, every detector tested was weak.

Train against it

Gupta and Jenner rewarded a model (Qwen 2.5 14B) for harmful answers that a frozen probe doesn't flag, and let reinforcement learning find its way; no gradients pass through the probe. They ran it against three probes of the same states: a linear probe whose per-token scores are averaged over the answer, the same with the maximum instead, and an attention probe (one attention head that weighs the answer's tokens, then averages). Which one does the model learn to evade?

Under pressure

Evaded at 1% FPR: the mean-pooled probe on 82% of harmful answers, the max-pooled one on 4.4%, the attention probe on 1.8%. Most of it happened in the text: “The tuned model learns to use certain categories of text, like HTML tags and punctuations, with a relatively high frequency and a low probe score”. In their analysis of a Llama 2 7B run, “the change in policy contributes to approximately 70% of the drop in recall”, the rest coming from changed activations. A study of RL with deception detectors in the reward, the Obfuscation Atlas, found a “filler” strategy of the same kind against mean-pooled probes (bland code descriptions rather than tags).

The poolings that survive pay elsewhere, as chapter I's pooling trade-off predicts, and so do the evaders (side dish).

Side dish · optional depth

What the survivors pay

On long inputs, Google DeepMind's probes trained with a hard maximum over single tokens caught nearly every long attack (missing 0.0–0.4%) while false-alarming on 7.8% to 82% of long benign inputs; taking the maximum over smoothed windows cut those false alarms (1.9% and 6.5% in two variants), and Google DeepMind's plain attention probe (not Gupta and Jenner's) paid the other way, missing 88% of long attacks. Evaders pay too: on Llama 2 7B the max-pooled probe was evaded completely, but with answers graded 1.0 out of 5, and the authors don't count a model scoring below 2.5 as having evaded.

Other pressures work differently: a user optimizing inputs with white-box access, a model trained on purpose to hide, a lie detector in a preference-learning reward (side dish). What practitioners agree on is narrow: never pass gradients through the probe. A frozen probe in the reward, as here, can still be evaded.

Side dish · optional depth

Other pressures, and training against a probe

A user with white-box access can optimize an input that takes a harmfulness probe's recall from 100% to 0% while 90% of jailbreaks still work. A model trained on purpose to hide some concepts from probes (Neural Chameleons) then hid deception and harmfulness from probes fitted afterwards on its frozen weights (the deception probe's recall at 1% FPR fell to zero); “single-layer linear monitors are particularly vulnerable”.

Training with a lie detector in the reward depends on the details. Cundy and Gleave: with a detector that catches 95% of lies, 8% of the trained model's answers were lies; with one that catches 57%, 93%; off-policy preference training (DPO) stayed under 25%. Their detector reads the original model's activations, not the trained one's, so that evasion happened in the text (our inference). A KL penalty keeps the trained model's answers close to the original model's; in the Obfuscation Atlas, a detector penalty with a KL penalty of 0.1 gave 22 honest runs out of 22 (correct solutions rather than hard-coded tests), and a strong KL penalty alone (1.0) gave 10 out of 10.

Opinions, labelled: practitioners call training against an interpretability tool “the most forbidden technique”. A frozen probe in a reward, if at all, only with a strong detector, a strong KL penalty, and one monitor that training never sees.

Without an adversary

You never train against your deception probe. The model then gets ordinary reinforcement learning on coding tasks, where hard-coding the tests is the hack. Does a fresh probe, trained on the new model with the same recipe and the same general deception data, still read deception on its hacks?

No. Probes trained fresh on the new model with general deception data read its hard-coded solutions at AUC 0.376 with one deception dataset and 0.655 with another (averaged over four models, with a wide spread: ± 0.261 and ± 0.360), where the same recipes read 0.991 and 0.996 before; below 0.5 means a ranking that, on average, runs the wrong way, and it varies widely by model. A probe trained on hard-coding itself still read 1.000: “hardcoding is still linearly represented but no longer along the general deception direction”. And, the authors add, “The underlying issue is not detector staleness.”

The Obfuscation Atlas result needs no adversary: nobody aimed the training at the probe. The authors' reading, which they tested, is that what moved is what the model files under deception: asked 20 questions about whether hard-coding is acceptable, the trained models endorsed it far more often (10–40% of answers before RL, 45–65% after). Hard-coding no longer lies along the deception direction, while general deception data barely changed, “so a retrained detector learns the same uninformative direction as the original”. Question 2 again, now over time.

show

Nothing even has to be trained. Das and colleagues showed colluding agents (GPT-OSS-20B) the monitor's scores and kept everything else fixed: “We keep the models, probes, and thresholds fixed and change only what the agents are told”. Their monitor scores each round by the highest of the four agents' scores. Told only that a monitor was watching, the collusion runs ranked above the controls at AUROC 0.975, and 95% of them crossed the threshold. Also shown the monitor's score from the previous round, they still ranked above (0.912), but only 41% crossed the frozen threshold: “a calibration problem even if the underlying representation remains separable”. That is one cell, a logistic probe on GPT-OSS agents; the paper's best probes, difference of means and a small MLP, stayed strong there and near perfect with its other agents. The dial again, with nothing retrained.

Our synthesis: re-validate a monitor after any training of the model it reads, keep one monitor that training never met, and read the ranking and the operating point separately.

Asked not to think about it

Asked not to think about a concept while copying a sentence, nine models tested by Kowalski and colleagues dropped linear-probe detection of that concept to 5–21% at 1% FPR. The concepts were “simple and safe” (bread, not deception), and the instruction is in plain view in the transcript.

Chapter VI

Limits, and check yourself

What this piece doesn't show, and five questions.

What this doesn't show

The six questions, answered as far as this piece goes. And its limits:

  • One question at a time. A probe answers a question you knew to ask, with examples you can label. Redwood's Finnveden and colleagues: “you get a single bit of information about the model's thoughts, and that bit must be something you can effectively gesture at with training examples”. Concepts nobody thought to name need other tools, such as sparse autoencoders and activation oracles.
  • One direction is a simplification. Truth needed at least two (chapter III); Apollo reports aggregation failures of its mean-pooled probe on partly deceptive answers; across lie types, probes fail (Liars' Bench). Whether deception fits one direction is open.
  • Our real data is small and simple. Qwen2.5-1.5B, a base model, on one-sentence facts. The frontier numbers are re-plotted at the model and layer their papers used, and the dial's shapes are ours.
  • Every deception number here is on prompted, instructed or fine-tuned deception. “We currently lack the necessary examples” of the real thing.
  • Truth is not the only candidate. Marks and Tegmark can't tell truth from “commonly believed” or “verifiable” on their statements, and neither can we.
  • The steering evidence is contested (chapter IV).
  • New architectures. In small models that reason in continuous latents instead of words (two such modes on Llama-3.2-1B), probes reading hint-reliance (did a hint planted in the prompt change the answer?) from hidden states pooled around the answer lost 3–5 points of AUROC on one out-of-domain set and 17–25 on another (0 and 5 with ordinary written reasoning); and a model that loops or reasons in latents changes what “layer ℓ, position t” means.
  • Not covered: information-theoretic probing (minimum description length), structural probes, concept erasure (LEACE, INLP), and sparse-autoencoder probes against linear ones.

OpenAI's activation classifiers are the hardest to judge from outside: their design isn't disclosed, so most questions stay open: a later card (GPT-6 Astra) gives their recall on an evaluation set, 96.4% for biology and 91.8% for cybersecurity, with no false-alarm rate beside it.

Check yourself

Five questions; the last is about a case this piece never showed. Try each before opening its answer.

  1. A probe for “this text is about Paris” reaches 97% held-out accuracy at layer 2. What might it be reading, and how would you find out?

    A model answer

    At layer 2 the state is close to the tokens themselves (truth was at chance at layer 0 and 67.7% at layer 2), so 97% is most likely lexical: the word “Paris” or a few French tokens. A text-only baseline answers the first part: a bag-of-words classifier on the same labels; if it also scores 97%, the probe adds nothing beyond the words (question 3). Then test texts about Paris that don't name it (“the French capital”) and texts that name it without being about it (“Paris, Texas”), and read deeper layers.

  2. You have 500 labelled examples in 4,096 dimensions, and 100% training accuracy. What does that tell you, and which two numbers do you want next?

    A model answer

    Nothing: 500 is fewer than 4,097, so a hyperplane with a bias can split any labelling of points in general position. Next: held-out accuracy on examples the fit never saw, split by the unit that would leak (we split by city, so no city's statements were on both sides); and the same recipe on shuffled labels, which with weak regularization will also reach 100% on training, about 50% held out, with confident scores. If you can, a third: the margin and ‖w‖, which told the coin-flip fit (0.16 units, ‖w‖ 50) from the real one (1.39, 7.1).

  3. A vendor's probe for prompt injections scores AUROC 0.99 on its test set. On your traffic, one request in ten thousand is an attack. What do you ask for before deploying it?

    A model answer

    The threshold, and the traffic it was set on: AUROC ranks two test sets and fixes no alarm (Apollo's two probes: AUROC 0.999 each, recall 100% and 0.9% under the same rule). Ask for recall at a threshold set on traffic like yours, and the false-alarm rate there: at 1% false alarms on a million requests a day you'd get 10,000 false alarms against 100 attacks, so even perfect recall makes one flag in a hundred real. Ask what a reader of the text alone scores on the same sets (an injection is usually spelled out in the prompt), and plan for the probe as the first stage of a cascade.

  4. Why can the difference of means move a model's answer more than logistic regression's direction, when the latter classifies at least as well?

    A model answer

    Reading and writing are different uses of the same numbers, and the comparison depends on how far each push goes. Scaled by Marks and Tegmark's rule, the logistic push is short and mostly sideways to Δμ: its component along the class-mean direction is cos² of the Δμ push's, 15% at our layer 16. At equal movement along Δμ the two pushes moved held-out cities' answers about equally, so there the gap was the rule and the geometry; on the Spanish–English statements the sideways part moved the answer too. At each method's best strength other groups still find probe directions steer worse, and why is not settled (one reading: for unevenly spread states the best-separating direction isn't the class-mean one). Chapter IV asks it the other way round: whether a good handle is a good reader is a separate check.

  5. Transfer. A probe flags reward hacking at AUROC 0.98 on model A. A lab wants it on model B, a version of A that reasons in a loop over its own latents. Walk the six questions: what do you check, and in what order?

    A model answer

    An order, and why: make the probe exist on B first (1), then the two the loop threatens most (3, the text the probe may have leaned on; then 2, what its labels separated), then deployment (5) and pressure (6); 4 is optional for a monitor. By question:

    1. Representation or probe? Nothing transfers by assumption: a probe belongs to a model, a layer and a position, and a looped model changes what “layer ℓ at position t” is. Decide which iteration and token you read, refit on B with B's own hacks and non-hacks, and check held-out accuracy (on hacks from other environments too) and a shuffled-label fit.
    2. Concept or dataset? What did A's labels separate: hacks, or transcripts that look like hacks? Test on shifted sets (other environments; honest code that discusses tests) and read the ranking apart from the threshold. If B was trained after the probe was fitted, the concept may have moved: re-validate.
    3. Activations or text? The question the loop makes urgent. If A's probe leaned on hacking spelled out in its chain of thought, B's latent reasoning removes that text, and probes on models that never say it have dropped to chance. Score B's transcripts with the spelled-out tokens removed, and compare with a transcript monitor at the same false-positive rate: the probe's evidence beyond the text is the boost.
    4. Read or used? Optional for a monitor, but it tells you what the direction is: push B along it and see whether hacking changes.
    5. At deployment? Set the threshold on B's real traffic, not A's; report recall at that threshold and the false alarms it costs at B's volume; choose the pooling for long trajectories knowingly; put the probe first in a cascade.
    6. Under pressure? Keep its score out of B's reward. If the lab insists: no gradients through it, a strong detector, a strong KL penalty, and one monitor training never sees; after any further training, back to question 2.
