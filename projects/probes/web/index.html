What a Probe Reads

A linear probe is a direction through a model's hidden states, and a threshold. Finding one that reads a concept at 99% takes minutes. Knowing what it actually reads takes more.

From a statement to a data point

A probe is a classifier, so it starts with a dataset. Take The city of Krasnodar is in Russia. The model splits it into 11 tokens and computes, for each token and at each of its 28 layers, a hidden state: a vector of 1,536 numbers, the residual stream. The glass draws that grid, one column per token and one dot per state.

We read one of those states, the one at layer 12 over the final token (the period), and call it h. It is stored with the statement's label y, 1 for true and 0 for false, as one row of a table. Do the same for every statement about the training cities, 1,026 of them, and you have a matrix X with one row per statement and 1,536 columns, and its labels y. A probe is a classifier fitted to (X, y).

statement

Switch to the false statement and only the last two columns are computed again: up to “in”, the two statements are the same input, and each state is computed from its own token and the tokens before it. That will matter two steps from now.

Where the statements come from Every city in the GeoNames gazetteer (CC BY 4.0) with more than 500,000 people, a name no other city shares, in a UN member state: 1,026 cities in 123 countries, each once with its country and once with a wrong one, drawn as often as that country appears in the true statements, so a country's name says nothing about truth (Marks and Tegmark's recipe). The columns and layers are a sketch; h and the rows of X are the model's real numbers, and the table draws every 12th number of each row.

A direction and a threshold

Each point is one statement's h, the data point from the last step: the hidden state at layer 12 over its final token, 1,536 numbers. The statements are about cities the probe was not trained on. Filled points are true statements; hollow ones are false.

A linear probe is a weight vector w and a bias b. It scores a statement as w·h + b and calls it true when the score is positive. The score depends only on where h projects onto the direction ŵ = w/‖w‖: every state on a hyperplane perpendicular to w gets the same score. The gold lines are those level sets, one logit apart, and the heavy one is the decision boundary.

Here w and b are what logistic regression learns from 1,026 labelled statements about other cities. The view is turned so that ŵ points right: a statement's horizontal position is its projection onto ŵ. The paper under the glass collects those projections on the same axis, in the grid's units, measured from the boundary: filled bars for true statements, outlined bars for false, each class scaled to sum to one.

Of the 1,026 statements about cities the probe never saw, it reads 744 correctly: 99.5%.

Honest geometry The plane holds ŵ (across) and the direction of largest remaining variance (up: the top singular vector of the centred states once ŵ is projected out). Across is exact; up is one choice among 1,535. At this layer it mostly separates statements involving China: the upper cluster of true statements is about Chinese cities. Which token?

Why read the period? In this model, as in every GPT-style model, attention is causal: the state over a token is computed from that token and the ones before it, never from the ones after. The state over “in” can't know how the statement ends. Here that is exact: a city's true and false statements are the same input up to “in”, so their states there are identical, and a probe trained to read “in” gets 50.0% of new statements right. No amount of data would help it.

The period is the first token after the country, the only one whose state has seen the whole statement. Marks and Tegmark found where a model keeps a statement's truth by patching, copying states from a true statement into a false one and watching the model's verdict: the states that carry it sit over the statement's last word and its period.

read

A probe can also read every token: give each token the statement's label, train on all of them, and pool the scores of a statement's tokens into one verdict. The paper shows those scores for the two Krasnodar statements, identical until the country and apart after it. Pooled by the mean, they read … of new statements correctly, as well as the period alone (…). Pooled by the maximum, …: some token before the country scores a little above zero (in Krasnodar it is “ras”, at 0.7), so a false statement's maximum stays above the boundary and it reads true. Even with a threshold of its own, set on the training statements, the maximum reads only …: whichever token scores highest decides the verdict, whether or not it says anything about truth.

For short statements that end in a period, the period is the natural place to read. For long transcripts, where what you want to detect can occupy a few tokens anywhere, probes score every token and pool. Anthropic's production probe smooths its per-token scores with a moving average, so that one spurious token can't flag a whole exchange; Google DeepMind's takes a hard maximum, so that a short harmful passage isn't diluted by a long context; McKenzie and colleagues found that probes reading only the last token “consistently” performed poorly on their conversations. Where a probe reads decides what it can see, and the monitoring chapter comes back to it.

A perfect fit proves nothing

Why trust that number? Not because the probe fits the statements it learned from. Take 300 statements, give them coin-flip labels so that there is nothing to find, and fit the same kind of probe: layer 16, almost no regularization. Gold rings mark the statements it was fitted to.

What training accuracy does it reach on coin-flip labels?

100%. In d dimensions a hyperplane with a bias can split any d + 1 points in general position every possible way, and 300 statements in 1,536 dimensions are far below that.

So the test has to be on new statements, and there the coin-flip probe scores 50.3%: chance. Yet it scores them with near-certainty, a median of 9 logits from the boundary. It found a direction along which the 300 statements barely differ (new ones sit a median 0.21 units from its boundary, against 3.4 for a probe fitted to true labels) and scaled w up until those slivers became large scores: ‖w‖ = 43 against 5.2. Both halves of that are visible at one scale: the coin-flip statements fill a sliver of the axis, and the gold level sets across it are 10 logits apart, where the true-label probe's are 1 logit apart.

labels show

With true labels, the same recipe reaches 100% on training statements and 99.6% on new ones. Only the held-out number tells the two probes apart.

Two different controls Shuffled labels ask whether fitting means anything here (it doesn't). Hewitt and Liang's control tasks ask something subtler: give each word type its own fixed random label and measure how much a probe scores by memorizing which word is which. Selectivity is task accuracy minus that control score.

Side dish · optional depth

How many points can a hyperplane split every way?

Cover (1965) counted the labellings of n points in general position that a hyperplane through the origin of ℝd can realize: all 2n of them while n ≤ d, and a share that falls through ½ at n = 2d and then to zero. A bias is one more dimension (append a constant 1 to every point), so a probe w·h + b in 1,536 dimensions splits any 1,537 points every way. That is the VC dimension of affine hyperplanes, d + 1.

Our 300 training statements in 1,536 dimensions sit at n/d ≈ 0.2, where every labelling fits. A perfect training score says nothing there; around n ≈ 2d it would start to. math, not data

Two directions that both read truth

A truth direction doesn't need an optimizer. Average the true statements' hidden states, average the false ones', and subtract: Δμ = μtrue − μfalse, the difference of means (Marks and Tegmark's “mass-mean” probe). With its threshold halfway between the two means it reads 96.0% of new statements correctly.

Yet Δμ and logistic regression's w point 46° apart in the full 1,536-dimensional space. The figure's plane contains both and is drawn in true proportions, so the angle you see is the real one.

What connects them is the L2 penalty. With a strong penalty, logistic regression returns Δμ: the penalized solution shrinks toward the loss gradient at w = 0, which points along μtrue − μfalse. As the penalty weakens, w turns away from Δμ, toward directions along which each class varies less. With no penalty it approaches the maximum-margin direction, since 1,026 statements in 1,536 dimensions are linearly separable.

strong: returns Δμweak: toward max-margin

Along the whole path the probe reads between 96.0% and 99.5% of new statements while it turns through 54°. Accuracy doesn't pin down a direction: a wide cone of directions separates true from false almost equally well, and each estimator, and each setting of it, picks its own.

So which of these is the model's truth direction?

Accuracy can't say. Whether the model uses a direction is a causal question: push the state along it and watch the output. That comes later, and the two directions come apart there.

Say “not”

Take the layer-12 probe from Fig. 2, 99.5% on new affirmative statements, and give it The city of Krasnodar is not in Russia. along with the other 1,025 negated statements about the same held-out cities. What share does it read correctly?

Upside down

Watch it in two moves. First the truth values flip in place: filled points turn hollow and hollow ones fill. Then each point travels to the hidden state of its negated twin.

Most stay on their side: 68.2% of the negated statements land on the same side of the boundary as their affirmative twins, and the probe reads 69.0% of them wrong.

Not noisily wrong: systematically. Rank the negated statements by score and the false ones come out on top, an AUROC of 0.006, where 0.5 means no signal and 0 a perfectly inverted ranking.

Our reading: among affirmative statements, “true” coincides with “this city belongs to this country”, and that match is what the probe learned. “Not” flips the truth and leaves the match in place. Marks and Tegmark proposed a similar confound, a feature for “close association”; the last figure shows the evidence in this model.

layer

Layer 8 looks like layer 12: 18.8% read correctly, AUROC 0.06. At layer 16 the probe calls every negated statement false. That is right for the false half, so accuracy is 50% and looks like guessing, but the ranking is still inverted: AUROC 0.19. The difference of means does the same at layer 12 (49.8%, AUROC 0.02). A threshold can hide which way a direction points; the ranking can't.

Retrain on both, and what that doesn't fix

Refit logistic regression on affirmative and negated statements from the training cities. The direction turns 65°, and it reads held-out statements at 98.5% (affirmative) and 98.1% (negated).

layer show

Now a different kind of fact: The Spanish word 'uno' means 'one'. At layer 12 the retrained probe reads these at 92.3% but their negations at only 66.2% (AUROC 0.76). At layer 16 it ranks both well (AUROC 0.99 and 0.95) yet reads them at only 68.3% and 62.5%: the direction carries over, and the threshold, set on cities, doesn't. There the affirmative-only probe had read the plain words at 92.3% and their negations at 50.0%.

Retraining fixes the shift you trained on, negation, for cities. It promises nothing about the next shift, and it can cost elsewhere: at layer 16, plain Spanish words fell from 92.3% to 68.3%.

Truth has at least two directions

Why does retraining on both work? Take the difference of means separately for affirmative statements, Δaff, and for negated ones, Δneg, and split them into a shared part and a flipping part: Δaff = g + p and Δneg = g − p, so g = ½(Δaff + Δneg) and p = ½(Δaff − Δneg).

In the plane of g and p the four kinds of statement form four groups. Truth runs along g for both polarities: a direction along g alone reads 96.8% of affirmative and 90.6% of negated statements. Along p, the upper groups are true “is in” and false “is not in”: exactly the statements where city and country match. That is the evidence for our reading of the flip.

The affirmative-only probe carries as much p as g (cosines 0.46 and 0.46), and “not” reverses the p part. The retrained probe has almost none (0.01). Bürger, Hamprecht and Nadler (2024) found this two-dimensional structure, a general truth direction and a polarity-sensitive one, in LLaMA3-8B and several other models from 7B to 27B parameters.
