What a Probe Reads

A linear probe is a direction through a model's hidden states, and a threshold. Finding one that reads a concept at 98% takes minutes. Knowing what it actually reads takes more.

From a statement to a data point

A probe is a classifier, so it starts with a dataset. Take The city of Krasnodar is in Russia. The model splits it into 11 tokens and computes a hidden state for each token at every depth: the embedding (layer 0), then the output of each of its 28 blocks. Each state is a vector of 1,536 numbers, the residual stream. The dark window, the glass, is the model's interior: one column per token, one dot per state.

We read one of those states, the one at layer 12 over the final token, the period (why those two, in the next steps), and call it h. It is stored with the statement's label y, 1 for true and 0 for false, as one row of a table. The light part of the figure is paper: what we read out of the model and can work with. Do the same for every statement about the 513 training cities, two per city, and you have a matrix X with 1,026 rows and 1,536 columns, and its labels y. A probe is a classifier fitted to (X, y).

Switch to the false statement, “…in China.” How many of the 11 columns get new states?

The last two, the country's and the period's. Each state is computed from its own token and the tokens before it, so up to “in” the two statements are the same input and have the same states, and the period's state reads the new country.

statement

In h's chip, blue is positive and grey negative, brighter for larger values. Its 1,536 numbers are folded into 12 rows of 128, and each row of X shows every 12th number, h's top row. The vertical stripes in X are coordinates that are large in almost every statement, true or false alike (the average true row and the average false row correlate at 0.99): you can't see truth in the numbers by eye.

Where the statements come from Every city in the GeoNames gazetteer (CC BY 4.0) with more than 500,000 people and a name no other city shares, in a UN member state, leaving out city-states and cities named after their country: 1,026 cities in 123 countries, split at random into two halves, one for training and one held out. Each city appears once with its country and once with a wrong one, drawn as often as that country appears in the true statements, so a country's name says nothing about truth (Marks and Tegmark's recipe). The columns and layers are a sketch; h and the rows of X are the model's real numbers.

A direction and a threshold

Each point in the glass is one statement's h, its state at layer 12 over the period. These are the 1,026 statements about the held-out cities, which the probe never saw. Filled points are true statements; hollow ones are false.

A linear probe is a weight vector w and a bias b. It scores a statement as w·h + b and calls it true when the score is positive. The score depends only on where h projects onto the direction ŵ = w/‖w‖: every state on a hyperplane perpendicular to w gets the same score. The gold lines are those level sets, one logit apart, and the heavy one is the decision boundary.

Here w and b are what logistic regression learns from the training statements. The view is turned so that ŵ points right, so a statement's horizontal position is its projection onto ŵ. The paper under the glass is that projection: the same axis, in h's own units (the grid's), measured from the boundary, with one histogram per class, filled for true and outlined for false, each scaled to sum to one.

Of the 1,026 new statements, it reads 1,010 correctly: 98.4%.

Honest geometry The plane holds ŵ (across) and the direction of largest remaining variance (up: the top singular vector of the centred states once ŵ is projected out). Across is exact; up is one choice among 1,535. At this layer it mostly separates statements involving China: the upper cluster of true statements is about Chinese cities. The grid is in the hidden state's own units and the horizontal axis is stretched, so its cells are 8.6× wider than tall. In true proportions the cells are squares, and truth is a narrow direction beside the largest ways these states vary.

Which token?

Why read the period? In this model, as in every GPT-style model, attention is causal: the state over a token is computed from that token and the ones before it, never from the ones after. So the state over “in” can't know how the statement ends. Hover a column in the glass to see which states it was computed from.

Train a probe on the states over “in”, from the same training statements. What share of new statements does it read correctly?

Exactly 50.0%. A city's true and false statements are the same input up to “in”, so their states there are identical: whatever the probe says about one, it says about the other, and it gets one of each pair right. No amount of data helps.

The paper shows the same test at every position: a probe trained on the states at that token, tested on the held-out cities. Up to “in” every position is at exactly 50%. The country's first token already reads 97.5%, its last token 98.8%, and the period 98.4%. Truth becomes readable the moment the country has been read, and stays readable.

read at

So why the period rather than the country? One reason is practical: the period is one fixed position in every statement, while a country takes one to four tokens. The other comes from Marks and Tegmark. They patched states from a true statement into a false one in LLaMA-2-13B, under a few-shot true/false prompt, and found causally important states over the statement's last token and its period, which they hypothesize hold its truth value. For their pictures they chose the period, because over the last word the states' main variation is the word itself (“clusters for statements ending in ‘China’ regardless of their truth value”).

A probe belongs to its position The probe of Fig. 2, trained on periods, scores the first token's state at −4,807 logits: in this model the first position's state is about 200 times larger than the others'. A probe is a measurement on the distribution it was trained on.

Or every token, pooled?

You could also read every token and combine them. Averaging comes first to mind, and for a linear probe it doesn't matter whether you average the states or the scores: w·mean(h) + b = mean(w·h + b). A probe trained on each statement's mean state reads 97.9% of new statements. The other design scores every token: give each token the statement's label, train on all of them, and pool each statement's token scores into one verdict.

The paper shows those per-token scores for the Krasnodar pair. Up to “in” they are identical and close to zero: each of those states appears once with each label, so the best the fit can do there is sit on the fence. Pooled by the mean, the statements read 98.5%, as well as the period.

Now pool by the maximum, with a threshold chosen on the training statements. What share of new statements does it read correctly?

87.8%. In 118 of the 513 false statements, a token before the country scores above the maximum's threshold and decides the verdict (toggle to …, where it is “…”). Keep the per-token threshold of zero instead and the maximum reads 50.0%: every false statement has some early token above zero (in Krasnodar, “ras”, at 0.7).

pool by statement

For short statements that end in a period, reading the period is the simple choice. Pooling matters for long inputs, where what you want to detect can occupy a few tokens anywhere, and the probes that pool by a maximum there are built for it (side dish). Where a probe reads decides what it can see; the piece comes back to this when probes become monitors.

Side dish · optional depth

How probes on long inputs pool

Probes that take a maximum are trained through it, or smooth first. Gupta and Jenner trained their max-pooled probes on the top-scoring tokens, because trained per token, as here, they reached “zero recall up to an FPR of 20%”. Anthropic's production probe, in Constitutional Classifiers++, scores every token, averages the logits over a sliding window in training and with an exponential moving average at inference, and escalates when the smoothed score crosses a threshold: “Without averaging, a single token with a spuriously high activation could incorrectly flag an entire sequence as harmful.” Google DeepMind's selected probe takes the maximum over attention-weighted averages of 10-token windows; one variant, MultiMax, takes a hard maximum over single tokens so that a short harmful passage isn't diluted by a long context. And on long conversations McKenzie and colleagues found that the probe reading only the last token “consistently performs poorly”.

Which layer?

A probe reads one layer, too, and so far it has been layer 12 of 28. Scrub through the model: each frame is one layer, drawn in that layer's own plane (its probe's direction across, the largest remaining variance up), with a probe trained at that layer on the same statements.

At layer 0, before any block has run, what does the cloud look like?

A single point. Layer 0 is the embedding of the token itself, and every statement's final token is the same “.”, so all 1,026 states are identical and no probe can beat chance there.

Truth becomes readable gradually, and then all at once: 67.7% at layer 2, 90.4% at layer 6, 95.0% at layer 10, and at layer 11 a jump to 98.5%, where it stays to the last layer (98.2%). Layer 12 sits just past the jump. The states keep growing with depth, which is why the grid rescales from frame to frame.

What a fit proves

Why trust Fig. 2's 98.4%? Not because the probe fits the statements it learned from. Take 300 training statements, give them coin-flip labels so there is nothing to find, and fit the same kind of probe, at layer 16 and with almost no regularization (C = 10⁴; C is the inverse of the L2 penalty's weight). It fits all 300: in d dimensions a hyperplane with a bias can split any d + 1 points in general position every possible way, and 300 is far below 1,537. The figure shows the fitted statements first, ringed in gold, then new ones.

On new statements it scores 51.1%: chance. How sure is it of those verdicts?

Near-certain: new statements sit a median of 7 logits from its boundary, a confidence above 99.9%, and half of those verdicts are wrong.

It found a direction along which the 300 statements barely differ (new ones sit a median of 0.15 units from its boundary, against 3.4 for a probe fitted to true labels) and scaled w up until those slivers became large scores: ‖w‖ = 50 against 7.1. At one scale you can see both: the coin-flip statements fill a sliver of the axis, and its gold level sets are 10 logits apart, where the true-label probe's are 2 logits apart (toggle the labels).

labels show

With true labels, the same recipe reaches 100% on training statements and 98.1% on new ones. Training accuracy can't tell the two probes apart. Held-out accuracy can, and so can the shape of the fit: a margin of 0.16 units against 1.39, and ‖w‖ of 50 against 7.1.

Two different controls Coin-flip labels ask whether fitting means anything here (it doesn't). Hewitt and Liang's control tasks ask something subtler: give each word type its own fixed random label and measure how much a probe scores by memorizing which word is which. Selectivity is task accuracy minus that control score.

Side dish · optional depth

How many points can a hyperplane split every way?

Cover (1965) counted the labellings of n points in general position that a hyperplane through the origin of ℝd can realize: all 2n of them while n ≤ d, and a share that falls through ½ at n = 2d and then to zero. A bias is one more dimension (append a constant 1 to every point), so a probe w·h + b in 1,536 dimensions splits any 1,537 points in general position every way. That is the VC dimension of affine hyperplanes, d + 1.

Our 300 training statements in 1,536 dimensions sit at n/d ≈ 0.2, where every labelling fits. A perfect training score says nothing there; around n ≈ 2d it would start to. math, not data

Two directions that both read truth

A truth direction doesn't need an optimizer. Average the true statements' hidden states, average the false ones', and subtract: Δμ = μtrue − μfalse, the difference of means (Marks and Tegmark's “mass-mean” probe; with balanced classes, as here, the same as their centred, unbiased version). With its threshold halfway between the two means it reads 95.4% of new statements correctly; logistic regression's w, 98.4%.

How far apart do Δμ and w point, in the full 1,536 dimensions?

About 60°: 57°. The figure's plane contains both and is drawn in true proportions, so the angle you see is the real one.

What connects them is the L2 penalty. On raw, centred activations with the bias unpenalized, a strong penalty makes logistic regression return Δμ: the penalized solution shrinks toward the loss gradient at w = 0, which points along μtrue − μfalse. (Standardize each coordinate first and the limit becomes Δμ rescaled coordinate by coordinate.) As the penalty weakens, w turns away from Δμ, roughly toward directions along which each class varies less. With no penalty it approaches the maximum-margin direction, since 1,026 statements in 1,536 dimensions are linearly separable.

strong: returns Δμweak: toward max-margin

Along the whole path the probe reads between 95.3% and 98.5% of new statements while it turns through 64°. Accuracy doesn't pin down a direction: a wide cone of directions separates true from false almost equally well, and each estimator, and each setting of it, picks its own. Which of them the model uses is a causal question: push the state along each and watch the output. Later in the piece the two come apart there.

Say “not”

Take the layer-12 probe from Fig. 2, 98.4% on new affirmative statements, and give it The city of Krasnodar is not in Russia. along with the other 1,025 negated statements about the same held-out cities. What share does it read correctly?

What “not” does to it

Watch it in two moves. First the truth values flip in place: filled points turn hollow and hollow ones fill. Then each point travels to the hidden state of its negated twin (squares are negated statements).

Two things happen at once. The negations of false statements, which are now true, stay where their twins were, on the false side: 511 of 513 are read false. The negations of true statements, now false, mostly drift across to the false side too (316 of 513), which happens to be right. So the probe calls 80.6% of the negations false and reads 31.0% of them correctly. Krasnodar shows both: “…is not in China.” stays on the false side, wrongly, and “…is not in Russia.” drifts there, rightly.

Not noisily wrong: systematically. Rank the negated statements by score and the false ones come out on top, an AUROC of 0.010, where 0.5 means no signal and 0 a perfectly inverted ranking. Among negations, the direction points the wrong way.

Our reading: among affirmative statements, “true” coincides with “this city belongs to this country”, and the probe learned partly that match. “Not” flips the truth and leaves the match in place. Marks and Tegmark proposed a similar confound, a feature for “close association”; the last figure shows the evidence in this model.

layer

Layer 8 looks like layer 12: 18.8% read correctly, AUROC 0.06. At layer 16 the probe calls every negated statement false. That is right for the false half, so accuracy is 50% and looks like guessing, but the ranking is still inverted: AUROC 0.19. The difference of means at layer 12 also calls nearly every negation false (99.8%): 49.8% correct, AUROC 0.02. A threshold can hide which way a direction points; the ranking can't.

Retrain on both, and what that doesn't fix

Refit logistic regression on affirmative and negated statements from the training cities. The direction turns 65°, and it reads held-out statements at 98.5% (affirmative) and 98.1% (negated).

Now a different kind of fact: The Spanish word 'uno' means 'one'., from our own list of 265 common Spanish words, each once with its translation and once with another word's. At layer 16 the retrained probe ranks these well, better than the affirmative-only probe did (AUROC 0.99 against 0.98), yet reads only 68.3% of them correctly: the direction carries over, and the threshold, set on cities, doesn't. At layer 12 it reads the plain words at 92.3% but their negations at 66.2% (AUROC 0.76).

Retraining fixed the shift it trained on: negation, for cities. It promises nothing about the next shift, and a new topic can need a new threshold even where the direction carries over.

Truth has at least two directions

Why does retraining on both work? Take the difference of means separately for affirmative statements, Δaff, and for negated ones, Δneg, and split them into a shared part and a flipping part: Δaff = g + p and Δneg = g − p, so g = ½(Δaff + Δneg) and p = ½(Δaff − Δneg). These are the difference-of-means version of Bürger, Hamprecht and Nadler's two directions.

In the plane of g and p the four kinds of statement form four groups. Truth runs along g for both polarities: a direction along g alone reads 95.5% of affirmative and 87.4% of negated statements. p is built to point from the mismatched groups (false “is in”, true “is not in”) to the matched ones (true “is in”, false “is not in”, where city and country match), so the matched groups sit up by construction.

What isn't by construction: p is as long as g (9.3 against 8.0 units at layer 16) and separates the groups cleanly, and the affirmative-only probe leans on p about as much as on g (cosines 0.34 and 0.30), while the retrained probe has almost none (0.02). That is the evidence for our reading of the flip: the first probe partly read city–country match, and “not” reverses it. Bürger and colleagues found this two-dimensional structure, a general truth direction and a polarity-sensitive one, in LLaMA3-8B and several other models from 7B to 27B parameters.
